Mirrors and chambers

Every polytope on this page is built from the mirrors of reflectional symmetry of a regular polytope. The cube, for instance, has nine planes of symmetry, and its $48$ symmetries, the isometries that map it to itself, are the products of the reflections in them. These planes cut its surface into $48$ triangles, the chambers, one for each flag: a vertex, an edge containing it, and a face containing that edge. A polytope is regular when its symmetries take any flag to any other. In four dimensions a flag also has a cell, and the chambers are tetrahedra.

A chamber has its corners at the centres of its flag’s vertex, edge and face, and its walls are numbered by the corners opposite them: wall $0$ is opposite the vertex, wall $1$ opposite the centre of the edge, and wall $2$ opposite the centre of the face. The reflection $s_i$ in wall $i$ changes the flag’s element of dimension $i$ and keeps the others. These reflections generate all the symmetries, so the angles between the walls determine them [@coxeter1973, §5.4, §7.6]. The triangle beside the main picture, a tetrahedron in four dimensions, is one chamber, $C$.

Schläfli symbols and Coxeter diagrams

Two notations describe a regular polytope: its Schläfli symbol says how its faces fit together, and its Coxeter diagram records the angles between the walls of its chambers. A regular polyhedron has Schläfli symbol $\{p,q\}$ when its faces are regular $p$-gons and $q$ of them meet at each vertex, like the cube $\{4,3\}$. A regular four-dimensional polytope has Schläfli symbol $\{p,q,r\}$ when its three-dimensional faces, the cells, are copies of $\{p,q\}$ and $r$ of them meet along each edge, like the tesseract $\{4,3,3\}$.

Reflecting in wall $0$ and then in wall $1$ turns the flag through $2\pi/p$ about the centre of its face, a $p$-gon. This angle is twice the angle between the mirrors, so walls $0$ and $1$ meet at the angle $\pi/p$ [@coxeter1973, §3.1, §7.6]. In the same way walls $1$ and $2$ meet at $\pi/q$ and walls $2$ and $3$ at $\pi/r$, and the other pairs of walls are perpendicular. The Coxeter diagram has a node for each wall, and the nodes of two walls that meet at the angle $\pi/m$ are joined by a line when $m \ge 3$, labelled $m$ unless $m = 3$ [@coxeter1973, §5.6]; for $\{p,q,r\}$ it is a chain of three lines labelled $p$, $q$ and $r$. The number of nodes is the rank.

Wythoff’s construction

Wythoff’s construction makes a polytope from the mirrors and one point $x$ of the chamber $C$, the yellow handle beside the picture: the vertices of the polytope are the images of $x$ under all the symmetries [@wythoff1918; @coxeter1973, §5.7, §11.6]. If $x$ is not on wall $i$, it is joined by an edge to its mirror image $s_i x$, and the Coxeter diagram rings the node of wall $i$; these edges and their images are all the edges. If $x$ is equally far from all the walls that do not contain it, the edges all have the same length and the polytope is uniform [@coxeter1935].

The images of $x$ under the rotations alone, the products of an even number of reflections, are the vertices of a snub polytope, such as the snub cube; $x$ in one of the grey chambers next to $C$ gives one. The dots mark the points that give uniform polytopes and snubs with equal edges.

Sphere, plane and hyperbolic plane

Changing the angles of the chamber moves it from the sphere to the plane or the hyperbolic plane, and the polyhedra become tilings. The sliders $p$, $q$ and $r$ set the angles $\pi/p$, $\pi/q$ and $\pi/r$ of the triangle $C$. The reflections in its sides generate the triangle group $(p\;q\;r)$, whose images of $C$ tile the space it lies in; for $r = 2$ it is the group of $\{p,q\}$. The angles of a triangle add up to more than $\pi$ on the sphere, to $\pi$ in the plane and to less than $\pi$ in the hyperbolic plane, so $C$ lies on the sphere when $\tfrac1p + \tfrac1q + \tfrac1r > 1$, in the plane when the sum is $1$ [@coxeter1973, §5.2, §5.4], and in the hyperbolic plane when it is less. The hyperbolic plane is drawn in the Poincaré disc, where lines are arcs of circles perpendicular to the rim. Walls at the angle $\pi/\infty = 0$ are parallel and meet at a point at infinity: the centre of a face with infinitely many sides when $p = \infty$, and a vertex where infinitely many faces meet when $q = \infty$.

In the plane, $\{4,4\}$, $\{3,6\}$, $\{6,3\}$ and their relatives give ten of the eleven tilings by regular polygons in which a symmetry takes any vertex to any other; the eleventh, the elongated triangular tiling, comes from no triangle group [@grunbaum1987, Ch. 2].

Duals

The dual of a polyhedron has a vertex for each face and a face for each vertex, a vertex and a face of the dual meeting when the face and the vertex they come from do; here it is the polar reciprocal in the unit sphere [@coxeter1973, §2.2]. A symmetry takes any vertex of a polyhedron of Wythoff’s construction to any other, so the faces of its dual are congruent: the dual of the cuboctahedron is the rhombic dodecahedron, for example, and the dual of the icosidodecahedron is the rhombic triacontahedron [@coxeter1973, §2.7].

Four dimensions

In rank $4$ the same construction gives four-dimensional polytopes and honeycombs of three-dimensional space. Six Schläfli symbols give convex regular polytopes [@coxeter1973, §7.8]: the $5$-cell $\{3,3,3\}$; the tesseract $\{4,3,3\}$ and the $16$-cell $\{3,3,4\}$, dual to each other; the $24$-cell $\{3,4,3\}$, its own dual, with no analogue in other dimensions [@coxeter1973, Epilogue]; and the $120$-cell $\{5,3,3\}$ and the $600$-cell $\{3,3,5\}$, dual to each other.

For the other symbols the chamber lies in Euclidean or hyperbolic space, and copies of $\{p,q\}$ fill that space, with $r$ of them along each edge: a honeycomb. Those with bounded cells are the cubic honeycomb $\{4,3,4\}$ [@coxeter1973, §4.6] and, in hyperbolic space, $\{5,3,4\}$, $\{5,3,5\}$, $\{4,3,5\}$ and $\{3,5,3\}$ [@coxeter1968, Ch. 10], drawn in the Poincaré ball.

Petrie polygons

A Petrie polygon of a regular polyhedron is a skew polygon of its edges in which every two consecutive sides, but no three, lie in one face. This condition determines a Petrie polygon by two adjacent edges, and as the symmetries take any such pair to any other, it is unique up to symmetries. Its number of sides $h$ satisfies $\cos^2(\pi/h) = \cos^2(\pi/p) + \cos^2(\pi/q)$ [@coxeter1973, §2.6]. The product $c = s_0 s_1 s_2$ takes the flag’s vertex $v$ to the next in the Petrie polygon: its vertices are $v$, $cv$, $c^2v$ and so on. This $c$ is a turn through $2\pi/h$ about an axis followed by the reflection in the plane perpendicular to it [@coxeter1973, §5.9], so seen along the axis, the Petrie polygon is a regular $h$-gon.

In four dimensions every three consecutive sides of a Petrie polygon, but no four, lie in a Petrie polygon of one cell. Again, it is unique up to symmetries, and the product $c = s_0 s_1 s_2 s_3$ takes each vertex to the next [@coxeter1973, §12.4]. Now $c$ is made of turns in two perpendicular planes, the first through $2\pi/h$. Projected onto that plane, the Coxeter plane, the Petrie polygon is a regular $h$-gon [@coxeter1973, §13.3], and since $c$ maps every polytope made from the same mirrors to itself, the projection of any of them is unchanged by the turn through $2\pi/h$.

Penrose tilings

Penrose tilings are tilings of the plane by two kinds of tile, a thick and a thin rhomb or a kite and a dart, whose edges can be matched by a rule only in tilings that no translation maps to themselves [@penrose1974; @grunbaum1987, Ch. 10]. The rule is given by arcs of two colours on the tiles: where two tiles share an edge, each arc that reaches it from one side continues on the other side in the same colour.
