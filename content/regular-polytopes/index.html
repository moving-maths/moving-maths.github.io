A polytope is regular when its symmetries take any flag (a vertex, an edge at it, a face at that edge, and so on) to any other. Its symmetries are generated by reflections, and the images of one point under them are the vertices of the polytope, or of one of its relatives, depending on where the point lies.

Mirrors

A flag of a polytope is a vertex, an edge at it, a face at that edge, and so on. A polytope is regular when its symmetry group acts transitively on its flags. The mirrors of its reflections cut the sphere round it into simplices, one for each flag, its chambers, and the reflections $s_0, s_1, \dots$ in the walls of one chamber $C$ generate the symmetry group. Walls $i$ and $j$ meet at the angle $\pi/m_{ij}$, where $m_{01} = p$, $m_{12} = q$ and $m_{23} = r$ for the polytope with Schläfli symbol $\{p,q,r\}$, and $m_{ij} = 2$ for the other pairs [@coxeter1973, Ch. 5]. The Coxeter diagram has a node for each wall, and a line marked $m_{ij}$ between nodes $i$ and $j$ when $m_{ij} > 2$ (unmarked when $m_{ij} = 3$).

Wythoff’s construction starts from a point $x$ of $C$. Its images under the group are the vertices of a polytope, and $x$ is joined by an edge to its image $s_i x$ in each wall $i$ that it is off [@wythoff1918; @coxeter1973, Ch. 8]. The ringed nodes of the diagram are the walls that $x$ is off. At the corner of $C$ off wall $0$ the polytope is $\{p,q,\dots\}$ itself; elsewhere it is rectified, truncated, cantellated and so on, and it is uniform, with regular faces, when $x$ is at the same distance from every wall it is off. The images of $x$ under the rotations alone, the products of an even number of reflections, are the vertices of the snub. The chambers next to $C$ hold the same points: the images under the rotations of a point there are those of its mirror image in $C$, reflected.

Sphere, plane and hyperbolic plane

In rank three the chamber is a triangle with angles $\pi/p$, $\pi/q$ and $\pi/r$, where $r = 2$ for the group $[p,q]$ of $\{p,q\}$. When $\tfrac1p + \tfrac1q + \tfrac1r > 1$ their sum is more than $\pi$: the triangle is spherical, and its images tile the sphere, as for the five Platonic solids. Stereographic projection takes the tiling of the sphere to a tiling of the plane by arcs of circles, as the Poincaré disc shows a tiling of the hyperbolic plane. When $\tfrac1p + \tfrac1q + \tfrac1r = 1$ the triangle is Euclidean: $\{4,4\}$, $\{3,6\}$ and $\{6,3\}$ tile the plane, and so do their relatives, among them all eleven tilings of the plane by regular polygons with every vertex alike but one, the elongated triangular tiling [@grunbaum1987, Ch. 2]. For all other $p$, $q$ and $r$ the triangle is hyperbolic, and the tiling covers the hyperbolic plane [@coxeter1973, Ch. 4]. For $m_{ij} = \infty$ the walls $i$ and $j$ meet at infinity, and the faces round that corner are apeirogons, polygons with infinitely many sides.

Duals

The dual of a polyhedron whose vertices lie on the unit sphere has a vertex $n/d$ for each face $\{y : n\cdot y = d\}$, and a face for each vertex [@coxeter1973, Ch. 2]. The polyhedra of Wythoff’s construction have all their vertices alike, so their duals have all their faces alike. The dual of the cuboctahedron $\mathrm r\{4,3\}$ is the rhombic dodecahedron, and the dual of the icosidodecahedron $\mathrm r\{5,3\}$ the rhombic triacontahedron. Their faces are rhombs: round each vertex of $\mathrm r\{p,q\}$ two $p$-gons and two $q$-gons alternate, and the vertex’s two mirrors swap them. The dual of a tiling has a vertex in the middle of each tile.

Four dimensions

There are six convex regular polytopes in four dimensions: the $5$-cell $\{3,3,3\}$; the tesseract $\{4,3,3\}$ and the $16$-cell $\{3,3,4\}$, dual to each other; the $24$-cell $\{3,4,3\}$, which is its own dual and has no analogue in other dimensions; and the $120$-cell $\{5,3,3\}$ and the $600$-cell $\{3,3,5\}$, dual to each other [@coxeter1973, Ch. 7]. The polytope’s boundary, projected radially onto the $3$-sphere, tiles it with cells. Stereographic projection takes the $3$-sphere into space and keeps angles: cells keep their shapes, and grow towards the edge of the picture. Perspective from a point beyond the polytope draws it with straight edges, one cell round all the others; parallel projection overlays its two halves.

The same diagrams with $\tfrac1p$, $\tfrac1q$, $\tfrac1r$ smaller give honeycombs: the cubic honeycomb $\{4,3,4\}$ of Euclidean space, and four honeycombs of hyperbolic space by dodecahedra, cubes and icosahedra, drawn in the Poincaré ball [@coxeter1973, Ch. 4]. Rhombic dodecahedra fill space too, in the dual of the honeycomb of tetrahedra and octahedra.

Penrose tilings

Penrose’s tiles are a thick rhomb, with angles $72^\circ$ and $108^\circ$, and a thin one, with angles $36^\circ$ and $144^\circ$; or a kite and a dart [@penrose1974]. Arcs drawn on the tiles must run on across every edge. With this matching rule the tiles cover the plane, but never periodically. The tiles can be cut into pieces that make up another Penrose tiling, with tiles $\varphi = \tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2$ times smaller, so that zooming in or out shows the same kind of tiling at every scale. In every Penrose tiling there are $\varphi$ times as many thick rhombs as thin ones, or kites as darts [@grunbaum1987, Ch. 10]. The rhomb tilings are also the projections onto a plane of slices of the lattice $\ZZ^5$ [@debruijn1981].
