The symmetries of a regular polytope are generated by reflections in a few mirrors. The images of one point under them are the vertices of the polytope, or of one of its relatives, depending on where the point lies.

Symmetries

The triangle beside the picture, a tetrahedron in four dimensions, is one of the pieces into which the polytope’s planes of symmetry cut the sphere round it. The symmetries of a regular polytope are generated by the reflections in these planes: the $48$ symmetries of the cube are products of reflections in its nine planes of symmetry. The pieces are the chambers. A flag is a vertex, an edge containing it, a two-dimensional face containing that edge, and so on; each chamber has its corners at the centres of the elements of one flag, and a polytope is regular when its symmetries take any flag to any other.

Number the walls of one chamber $C$ so that wall $i$ is opposite the corner at the centre of the flag’s $i$-dimensional face. The reflections $s_i$ in the walls generate all the symmetries, so the angles between the walls determine them [@coxeter1973, §5.4, §7.6].

Schläfli symbols and Coxeter diagrams

The Coxeter diagram under the chamber records the angles between its walls: a node for each wall, and a line between the nodes of two walls that meet at the angle $\pi/m$ when $m \ge 3$, labelled $m$ unless $m = 3$ [@coxeter1973, §5.6]. The number of nodes is the rank.

For a regular polytope the angles come from its Schläfli symbol. A regular polyhedron is $\{p,q\}$ when its faces are regular $p$-gons and $q$ of them meet at each vertex, like the cube $\{4,3\}$. A regular four-dimensional polytope is $\{p,q,r\}$ when its three-dimensional faces, the cells, are copies of $\{p,q\}$ and $r$ of them meet along each edge, like the tesseract $\{4,3,3\}$. The product $s_0 s_1$ turns the flag’s $p$-gon through $2\pi/p$, and a product of two reflections turns through twice the angle between their mirrors, so walls $0$ and $1$ meet at $\pi/p$ [@coxeter1973, §3.1, §7.6]. Likewise walls $1$ and $2$ meet at $\pi/q$ and walls $2$ and $3$ at $\pi/r$, and the other walls are perpendicular: the diagram is a path labelled $p$, $q$, $r$.

Wythoff’s construction

The yellow point $x$ in the chamber chooses the polytope: its vertices are the images of $x$ under all the symmetries [@wythoff1918; @coxeter1973, §5.7, §11.6]. If $x$ is not on wall $i$, it is joined by an edge to its mirror image $s_i x$, and the diagram rings the node of wall $i$; these edges and their images are all the edges. With $x$ at the corner opposite wall $0$ the polytope is $\{p,q,\dots\}$, at the corner opposite the last wall its dual $\{\dots,q,p\}$, and elsewhere a relative named by the walls that contain $x$, such as the truncated cube [@coxeter1935]. If $x$ is equally far from the walls that do not contain it, all the edges have the same length and the polytope is uniform.

The images of $x$ under the rotations alone, the products of an even number of reflections, are the vertices of a snub polytope; in the picture, $x$ in the grey chamber across wall $i$ gives the mirror image of the snub of $s_i x$. The dots mark the uniform polytopes and the snubs with equal edges. With $x$ at a dot, the dots that give the same picture are ringed in blue, and those that give the same shape moved or scaled in grey.

Sphere, plane and hyperbolic plane

The sliders $p$, $q$ and $r$ set the angles $\pi/p$, $\pi/q$ and $\pi/r$ of the chamber, and with them the space it lies in. The reflections in the sides of such a triangle generate the triangle group $(p\;q\;r)$, whose images of the triangle tile that space; for $r = 2$ it is the group of $\{p,q\}$. The angles of a triangle add up to more than $\pi$ on the sphere, to $\pi$ in the plane and to less in the hyperbolic plane, so the triangle lies on the sphere when $\tfrac1p + \tfrac1q + \tfrac1r > 1$, in the plane when the sum is $1$ [@coxeter1973, §5.2, §5.4], and in the hyperbolic plane when it is less. The hyperbolic plane is drawn in the Poincaré disc, where lines are arcs of circles perpendicular to the rim. Walls at the angle $\pi/\infty = 0$ are parallel and meet at a point at infinity, where the faces have infinitely many sides.

In the plane, $\{4,4\}$, $\{3,6\}$, $\{6,3\}$ and their relatives give ten of the eleven tilings by regular polygons in which a symmetry takes any vertex to any other; the eleventh, the elongated triangular tiling, comes from no triangle group [@grunbaum1987, Ch. 2].

Duals

The dual of a polyhedron has a vertex for each face and a face for each vertex, a vertex and a face of the dual meeting when the face and the vertex they come from do; here it is the polar reciprocal in the unit sphere [@coxeter1973, §2.2]. A symmetry takes any vertex of a polyhedron of Wythoff’s construction to any other, so its dual has congruent faces. At a vertex of the cuboctahedron, for example, two triangles and two squares alternate, and the two mirrors through the vertex reflect the dual’s face there in its diagonals, so that face is a rhomb: the dual is the rhombic dodecahedron. In the same way the dual of the icosidodecahedron is the rhombic triacontahedron [@coxeter1973, §2.7].

Four dimensions

In rank $4$ the chamber is a tetrahedron, and $x$ gives a four-dimensional polytope or a honeycomb. Six Schläfli symbols give convex regular polytopes [@coxeter1973, §7.8]: the $5$-cell $\{3,3,3\}$; the tesseract $\{4,3,3\}$ and the $16$-cell $\{3,3,4\}$, dual to each other; the $24$-cell $\{3,4,3\}$, its own dual, with no analogue in other dimensions [@coxeter1973, Epilogue]; and the $120$-cell $\{5,3,3\}$ and the $600$-cell $\{3,3,5\}$, dual to each other.

In space their boundaries are drawn by projection. Stereographic projection, from a point of the $3$-sphere round the polytope, preserves angles and draws the cells near that point large; perspective projection, from a point outside, keeps the edges straight; parallel projection leaves out the fourth coordinate and draws the two halves of the boundary over each other.

For the other symbols the chamber lies in Euclidean or hyperbolic space, and copies of $\{p,q\}$ fill it, with $r$ of them along each edge: a honeycomb. Those with bounded cells are the cubic honeycomb $\{4,3,4\}$ [@coxeter1973, §4.6] and, in hyperbolic space, $\{5,3,4\}$, $\{5,3,5\}$, $\{4,3,5\}$ and $\{3,5,3\}$ [@coxeter1968, Ch. 10], drawn in the Poincaré ball.

Petrie polygons

A Petrie polygon of a regular polyhedron is a skew polygon of its edges in which every two consecutive sides, but no three, lie in one face. Its number of sides $h$ satisfies $\cos^2(\pi/h) = \cos^2(\pi/p) + \cos^2(\pi/q)$, and there are $g/2h$ of them, where $g$ is the number of symmetries [@coxeter1973, §2.6]. The product $c = s_0 s_1 s_2$ takes the flag’s vertex $v$ along one: its vertices are $v$, $cv$, $c^2v$ and so on. Here $c$ is a rotation through $2\pi/h$ about an axis followed by the reflection in the plane perpendicular to it [@coxeter1973, §5.9], so seen along the axis, the Petrie polygon is a regular $h$-gon.

In four dimensions every three consecutive sides of a Petrie polygon, but no four, lie in a Petrie polygon of one cell, and $c = s_0 s_1 s_2 s_3$ again takes each vertex to the next [@coxeter1973, §12.4]. Now $c$ is made of rotations in two perpendicular planes, the first through $2\pi/h$. Projected orthogonally to that plane, the Coxeter plane, the Petrie polygon is a regular $h$-gon [@coxeter1973, §13.3], and as $c$ maps the polytope to itself, the projection of any polytope made from the same mirrors is unchanged by that rotation.

Sections

The sections of a polytope are its intersections with parallel planes, or hyperplanes in four dimensions, as a solid passing through a plane meets it in a sequence of polygons [@coxeter1973, §13.1]. The pictures use the planes $u \cdot y = t$, with the centre at $0$, the farthest vertices at distance $1$, and $u$ the unit vector from the centre of a vertex, an edge, a face or a cell towards the centre. The section changes its combinatorial type only where it passes through vertices, and there those vertices alone span what Coxeter calls the simplified section. Between these levels the area of the section, or its volume, is a polynomial in $t$; its integral over $t$ is the volume of the polytope.

Penrose tilings

Penrose tilings are tilings of the plane by two kinds of tile, a thick and a thin rhomb or a kite and a dart, whose edges are matched by a rule that can be obeyed over the whole plane, but only by tilings that no translation maps to themselves [@penrose1974]. The rule is given by arcs of two colours on the tiles: where two tiles share an edge, the arcs that meet it from either side have the same colour.

Each tile can be cut into tiles and halves of tiles $\varphi = \tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2$ times smaller, and the halves from neighbouring tiles join up into a Penrose tiling with smaller tiles. Conversely, the tiles of a Penrose tiling and halves of them make up a unique Penrose tiling with larger tiles, whose edges run along the edges and the axes of symmetry of the smaller tiles. In a large disc there are about $\varphi$ times as many thick rhombs as thin ones, and about $\varphi$ times as many kites as darts [@grunbaum1987, Ch. 10].
