The symmetries of a regular polytope are generated by reflections in a few mirrors. The images of one point under them are the vertices of the polytope, or of one of its relatives, depending on where the point lies.

Symmetries

The symmetries of a polytope are the isometries that map it to itself. A regular polytope has as many as it can: a symmetry takes any flag, a vertex with an edge at it, a face at that edge and so on, to any other. The mirrors of the reflections among them cut the sphere round the polytope into small simplices, the chambers, one for each flag. The reflections $s_0, s_1, \dots$ in the walls of one chamber $C$ generate all the symmetries, so the angles between these walls determine the group [@coxeter1973, Ch. 5].

The Schläfli symbol of a regular polytope records these angles. The faces of $\{p,q\}$ are $p$-gons, $q$ at each vertex, and the cells of $\{p,q,r\}$ are $\{p,q\}$, $r$ round each edge. In $C$, walls $0$ and $1$ meet at the angle $\pi/p$, walls $1$ and $2$ at $\pi/q$, walls $2$ and $3$ at $\pi/r$, and the other pairs at right angles. The cube $\{4,3\}$, with three squares at each vertex, has chambers with angles $\pi/4$, $\pi/3$ and $\pi/2$.

The Coxeter diagram shows the same angles as a graph: a node for each wall, and a line between nodes $i$ and $j$ when the walls meet at an angle $\pi/m_{ij}$ less than a right angle, marked with $m_{ij}$ unless $m_{ij} = 3$. For $\{p,q,r\}$ it is a path with the marks $p$, $q$, $r$. Three walls can also bound a triangle with angles $\pi/p$, $\pi/q$ and $\pi/r$, all three less than a right angle; its reflections generate the triangle group $(p\;q\;r)$, and its diagram is a triangle.

Wythoff’s construction

To construct a polytope from the mirrors, choose a point $x$ of $C$. Its images under the group are the vertices, and $x$ is joined by an edge to its image $s_i x$ in each wall $i$ that it is off [@wythoff1918; @coxeter1973, Ch. 8]; the ringed nodes of the diagram are those walls. At the corner of $C$ off wall $0$ alone this gives the regular polytope $\{p,q,\dots\}$, and at the corner off the last wall alone $\{\dots,q,p\}$. Elsewhere it gives the rectified, truncated, cantellated, … polytopes, which are uniform, with regular faces, when $x$ is equally far from all the walls it is off.

Under the rotations alone, the products of an even number of reflections, the images of $x$ are the vertices of the snub. A point in a chamber next to $C$ gives the snub of its mirror image in $C$, reflected; at the far corner of that chamber it gives the regular polytope again.

Sphere, plane and hyperbolic plane

Where the mirrors live depends on their angles. A triangle with angles $\pi/p$, $\pi/q$ and $\pi/r$ has angle sum more than, equal to or less than $\pi$ as $\tfrac1p + \tfrac1q + \tfrac1r$ is more than, equal to or less than $1$, and accordingly it is a triangle of the sphere, the plane or the hyperbolic plane; its images under the group tile that space [@coxeter1973, Ch. 4]. On the sphere they give the five Platonic solids and their relatives. Stereographic projection takes the tiling of the sphere to a tiling of the plane by arcs of circles, as the Poincaré disc shows a tiling of the hyperbolic plane. In the plane, $\{4,4\}$, $\{3,6\}$, $\{6,3\}$ and their relatives give ten of the eleven tilings by regular polygons with every vertex alike; the eleventh, the elongated triangular tiling, comes from no triangle of mirrors [@grunbaum1987, Ch. 2]. Walls with $m_{ij} = \infty$ meet only at infinity, and the faces round that corner are apeirogons, polygons with infinitely many sides.

Duals

Exchanging the vertices and the faces of a polyhedron gives its dual, with a vertex for each face and a face for each vertex. When the vertices lie on the unit sphere, the face $\{y : n\cdot y = d\}$ gives the vertex $n/d$ [@coxeter1973, Ch. 2]. The polyhedra of Wythoff’s construction have all their vertices alike, so their duals have all their faces alike. Round each vertex of the cuboctahedron $\mathrm r\{4,3\}$ two squares and two triangles alternate, and the vertex’s two mirrors swap them, so the faces of its dual, the rhombic dodecahedron, are rhombs; in the same way the dual of the icosidodecahedron $\mathrm r\{5,3\}$ is the rhombic triacontahedron. The dual of a tiling has a vertex in the middle of each tile.

Four dimensions

With four mirrors the chamber is a tetrahedron, and the Schläfli symbol $\{p,q,r\}$ gives the angles between its walls. Six symbols give convex regular polytopes: the $5$-cell $\{3,3,3\}$; the tesseract $\{4,3,3\}$ and the $16$-cell $\{3,3,4\}$, dual to each other; the $24$-cell $\{3,4,3\}$, its own dual and with no analogue in other dimensions; and the $120$-cell $\{5,3,3\}$ and the $600$-cell $\{3,3,5\}$, dual to each other [@coxeter1973, Ch. 7]. To see one in space, project its boundary radially onto the $3$-sphere round it, where its cells tile the $3$-sphere, and then into space. Stereographic projection keeps angles: the cells keep their shapes, and grow towards the edge of the picture. Perspective from a point beyond the polytope draws it with straight edges, one cell round all the others; parallel projection overlays its two halves.

Other symbols give tetrahedra of Euclidean or hyperbolic space, whose images fill the space with cells: honeycombs. The regular ones with bounded cells are the cubic honeycomb $\{4,3,4\}$ of Euclidean space, and four honeycombs of hyperbolic space by dodecahedra, cubes and icosahedra, drawn in the Poincaré ball [@coxeter1973, Ch. 4]. Rhombic dodecahedra fill space too, in the dual of the honeycomb of tetrahedra and octahedra.

Penrose tilings

The tilings of the plane above all repeat in two directions. Penrose found pairs of tiles that cover the plane, but never periodically [@penrose1974]: a thick rhomb, with angles $72^\circ$ and $108^\circ$, and a thin one, with angles $36^\circ$ and $144^\circ$; or a kite and a dart. The arcs drawn on the tiles must run on across every edge, and this matching rule rules out the periodic tilings. Each tile can be cut into pieces that make up another Penrose tiling, with tiles $\varphi = \tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2$ times smaller, so that zooming in or out shows the same kind of tiling at every scale; the other way round, the tiles group into patches that make up a Penrose tiling with larger tiles. In every Penrose tiling there are $\varphi$ times as many thick rhombs as thin ones, or kites as darts [@grunbaum1987, Ch. 10]. The rhomb tilings are also the projections onto a plane of slices of the lattice $\ZZ^5$ [@debruijn1981].
