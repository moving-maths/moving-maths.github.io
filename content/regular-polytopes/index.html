The symmetries of a regular polytope are generated by reflections in a few mirrors. The images of one point under them are the vertices of the polytope, or of one of its relatives, depending on where the point lies.

Symmetries

The symmetries of a polytope are the isometries of space that map it to itself. For a regular polytope they are always generated by reflections: the $48$ symmetries of the cube, for instance, are the products of reflections in its nine planes of symmetry. The regular polytopes can in fact be defined by their symmetries. A flag of a polytope is a vertex, an edge containing that vertex, a two-dimensional face containing that edge, and so on, up to a face of one dimension less than the polytope; a polytope is regular when for any two of its flags there is a symmetry taking the first to the second.

The mirrors of the reflections among the symmetries cut the sphere round the polytope into spherical simplices, the chambers: triangles for a polyhedron, tetrahedra for a four-dimensional polytope. There is one chamber for each flag, with corners at the flag’s vertex and at the centres of its edge, its two-dimensional face and so on, projected to the sphere. Let $C$ be one chamber, and number its walls $0, 1, 2, \dots$ so that wall $i$ is opposite the corner at the centre of the flag’s $i$-dimensional face. The reflection $s_i$ in wall $i$ is the symmetry that replaces this face of the flag by another and keeps the rest of the flag. The reflections $s_0, s_1, \dots$ generate all the symmetries, so the angles between the walls of $C$ determine the group [@coxeter1973, §5.4, §7.6].

Schläfli symbols and Coxeter diagrams

The Schläfli symbol of a regular polytope says how its faces fit together. A regular polyhedron is $\{p,q\}$ when its faces are regular $p$-gons and $q$ of them meet at each vertex: the cube is $\{4,3\}$. A regular four-dimensional polytope is $\{p,q,r\}$ when its three-dimensional faces, the cells, are regular polyhedra $\{p,q\}$ and $r$ cells meet along each edge: the tesseract $\{4,3,3\}$ has cubes for cells, three along each edge.

The same numbers give the angles of the chambers. The reflections $s_0$ and $s_1$ map the flag’s two-dimensional face to itself and generate its symmetries, those of a regular $p$-gon, so their product is a rotation through $2\pi/p$. The product of two reflections is a rotation through twice the angle between their mirrors, so walls $0$ and $1$ meet at the angle $\pi/p$ [@coxeter1973, §3.1, §7.6]. In the same way walls $1$ and $2$ meet at $\pi/q$ and walls $2$ and $3$ at $\pi/r$, and the other pairs of walls are perpendicular. The chambers of the cube are triangles with angles $\pi/4$, $\pi/3$ and $\pi/2$.

The Coxeter diagram shows these angles as a graph, with a node for each wall of $C$. The nodes of two walls that meet at the angle $\pi/m$ are joined by a line when $m \ge 3$, labelled $m$ unless $m = 3$; the nodes of perpendicular walls are not joined [@coxeter1973, §5.6]. For $\{p,q,r\}$ the diagram is a path with the labels $p$, $q$ and $r$. The number of nodes, one more than the number of entries in the Schläfli symbol, is the rank of the group: $2$ for a polygon, $3$ for a polyhedron or a tiling of a plane, and $4$ for a four-dimensional polytope or a honeycomb of three-dimensional space.

Wythoff’s construction

To construct a polytope from the mirrors, choose a point $x$ of $C$, and take as vertices its images $gx$ under all the symmetries $g$ [@wythoff1918; @coxeter1973, §5.7, §11.6]. A wall $i$ of $C$ either contains $x$, and then $s_i x = x$, or it does not, and then $x$ is joined by an edge to its mirror image $s_i x$; these edges and their images under the symmetries are all the edges. The Coxeter diagram marks the walls that do not contain $x$ by rings round their nodes. When $x$ is the corner of $C$ opposite wall $0$, the vertex of the flag, the vertices are those of the regular polytope $\{p,q,\dots\}$; when $x$ is the corner opposite the last wall, they are those of its dual $\{\dots,q,p\}$. The other points of $C$ give relatives of these [@coxeter1935], named by the walls that contain $x$: rectified, truncated, cantellated and so on. All the edges have the same length when $x$ is equally far from the walls that do not contain it, and then every face is a regular polygon: the polytope is uniform.

The rotations among the symmetries, the products of an even number of reflections, make up half of them. The images of a point under the rotations alone are the vertices of a snub polytope, such as the snub cube, and for one point inside $C$ all its edges have the same length. In the picture of $C$, the three chambers next to it are grey: a point $x$ in the chamber across wall $i$ gives the mirror image in wall $i$ of the snub of the point $s_i x$ of $C$. At the far corner of that chamber, the mirror image of a corner of $C$, the rotations give the same images as all the symmetries, and the regular polytope appears again.

The dots in the picture of $C$ mark the points where $x$ gives a uniform polytope or a snub with equal edges, and the far corners of the grey chambers. With $x$ at a dot, the dots where $x$ gives exactly the same picture are ringed in blue, and those where it gives the same shape after a translation, rotation or scaling are ringed in grey.

Sphere, plane and hyperbolic plane

For any whole numbers $p, q, r \ge 2$ there is a triangle with angles $\pi/p$, $\pi/q$ and $\pi/r$, and the reflections in its sides generate a group, the triangle group $(p\;q\;r)$, whose images of the triangle tile the space it lies in. For $r = 2$ it is the group of $\{p,q\}$; when $p, q, r \ge 3$ its Coxeter diagram is a triangle. The angles of a triangle add up to more than $\pi$ on the sphere, to exactly $\pi$ in the plane and to less than $\pi$ in the hyperbolic plane, so the triangle lies on the sphere when $\tfrac1p + \tfrac1q + \tfrac1r > 1$, in the plane when the sum is $1$ [@coxeter1973, §5.2, §5.4] and in the hyperbolic plane when it is less than $1$. The numbers may also be $\infty$: two walls at the angle $\pi/\infty = 0$ are parallel and meet only at infinity, and the faces round that point at infinity are apeirogons, polygons with infinitely many sides.

On the sphere the groups $[p,q]$ give the five Platonic solids $\{3,3\}$, $\{4,3\}$, $\{3,4\}$, $\{5,3\}$ and $\{3,5\}$ and their relatives, drawn as polyhedra with flat faces, or by stereographic projection, which maps the sphere to the plane and its great circles to circles and lines. The Poincaré disc draws the hyperbolic plane in the same way, with its lines as arcs of circles perpendicular to the boundary. In the plane, $\{4,4\}$, $\{3,6\}$ and $\{6,3\}$ and their relatives give ten of the eleven tilings by regular polygons in which a symmetry takes any vertex to any other; the eleventh, the elongated triangular tiling, comes from no triangle group [@grunbaum1987, Ch. 2].

Duals

The dual of a polyhedron has a vertex for each face of the polyhedron and a face for each vertex, and a vertex and a face of the dual meet when the face and the vertex they come from do. Here it is the polar reciprocal in the unit sphere through the vertices: the face in the plane $n \cdot y = d$, with $n$ a unit vector, gives the vertex $n/d$, and the vertex $v$ gives the face in the plane $v \cdot y = 1$ [@coxeter1973, §2.2]. In the polyhedra of Wythoff’s construction a symmetry takes any vertex to any other, so in their duals a symmetry takes any face to any other, and all the faces are congruent.

At each vertex of the cuboctahedron $\mathrm r\{4,3\}$, two triangles and two squares alternate. One mirror through the vertex passes through the centres of the two triangles and the other through the centres of the two squares. The face of the dual for that vertex has a corner for each of these four faces, and the two mirrors reflect it in its two diagonals. A quadrilateral symmetric in both its diagonals is a rhomb, so the dual is the rhombic dodecahedron. In the same way the dual of the icosidodecahedron $\mathrm r\{5,3\}$ is the rhombic triacontahedron [@coxeter1973, §2.7]. The dual of a tiling has a vertex at the centre of each tile and a tile round each vertex.

Four dimensions

The mirrors of a regular four-dimensional polytope $\{p,q,r\}$ cut the $3$-sphere round it into chambers that are tetrahedra, whose walls meet at the angles that its Schläfli symbol gives. Six symbols give convex regular polytopes [@coxeter1973, §7.8]: the $5$-cell $\{3,3,3\}$; the tesseract $\{4,3,3\}$ and the $16$-cell $\{3,3,4\}$, dual to each other; the $24$-cell $\{3,4,3\}$, which is its own dual and has no analogue in other dimensions [@coxeter1973, Epilogue]; and the $120$-cell $\{5,3,3\}$ and the $600$-cell $\{3,3,5\}$, dual to each other.

To draw such a polytope in space, stereographic projection first projects its boundary radially onto the $3$-sphere round it, where the cells tile the $3$-sphere, and then maps the $3$-sphere to space from one of its points. It preserves angles, so each small cell keeps its shape, and cells near the point of projection are drawn large, towards the edge of the picture. Perspective projection maps the polytope itself to space from a point outside it: the edges stay straight, and the cell nearest that point is drawn round all the others. Parallel projection leaves out the fourth coordinate, so that the two halves of the boundary, where the fourth coordinate is positive and where it is negative, are drawn on top of each other.

For the other symbols the tetrahedron with these angles lies in Euclidean or hyperbolic space instead, and copies of the regular polyhedron $\{p,q\}$ fill that space, meeting face to face with $r$ of them along each edge: a honeycomb. The regular honeycombs with bounded cells are the cubic honeycomb $\{4,3,4\}$ of Euclidean space, with four cubes along each edge [@coxeter1973, §4.6], and four honeycombs of hyperbolic space: $\{5,3,4\}$ and $\{5,3,5\}$ by dodecahedra, $\{4,3,5\}$ by cubes and $\{3,5,3\}$ by icosahedra [@coxeter1968, Ch. 10]. They are drawn in the Poincaré ball, which shows hyperbolic space inside a ball, with its planes as pieces of spheres and planes perpendicular to the boundary. Rhombic dodecahedra fill Euclidean space too, as the cells of the dual of the honeycomb of tetrahedra and octahedra [@coxeter1973, §4.7].

Petrie polygons

A Petrie polygon of a regular polyhedron is a skew polygon made of its edges in which every two consecutive sides, but no three, lie in one face: it zigzags round the polyhedron. Its number of sides $h$ satisfies $\cos^2(\pi/h) = \cos^2(\pi/p) + \cos^2(\pi/q)$, so $h$ is $4$ for the tetrahedron, $6$ for the cube and the octahedron and $10$ for the dodecahedron and the icosahedron, and $\{p,q\}$ has $g/2h$ Petrie polygons, where $g$ is the number of its symmetries [@coxeter1973, §2.6]. The product $c = s_0 s_1 s_2$ of the three reflections maps the polyhedron to itself, and the Petrie polygon through the vertex $v$ of the flag and along its edge has the vertices $v$, $cv$, $c^2v$ and so on. Here $c$ is a rotation through $2\pi/h$ about an axis followed by the reflection in the plane perpendicular to that axis [@coxeter1973, §5.9], so that seen along the axis, the Petrie polygon is a regular $h$-gon.

In four dimensions a Petrie polygon is a skew polygon in which every three consecutive sides, but no four, lie in a Petrie polygon of one cell, and $c = s_0 s_1 s_2 s_3$ again takes each of its vertices to the next [@coxeter1973, §12.4]. Now $c$ is made of rotations in two perpendicular planes, through $2\pi/h$ in the first and through a multiple of it in the second. The orthogonal projection to the first plane, the Coxeter plane, maps the Petrie polygon to a regular $h$-gon [@coxeter1973, §13.3]: $h$ is $5$ for the $5$-cell, $8$ for the tesseract and the $16$-cell, $12$ for the $24$-cell and $30$ for the $120$-cell and the $600$-cell. Since $c$ maps the polytope to itself and turns the plane through $2\pi/h$, the projection of the polytope, or of any other polytope made from the same mirrors, is unchanged by that rotation.

Sections

A solid passing through a plane meets it in a sequence of polygons, and a four-dimensional polytope passing through a hyperplane meets it in a sequence of polyhedra [@coxeter1973, §13.1]. With the centre of the polytope at $0$ and its farthest vertices at distance $1$, the picture shows its sections by the hyperplanes $u \cdot y = t$, where $u$ is the unit vector from the centre of a vertex, an edge, a two-dimensional face or a cell towards the centre of the polytope, so that the first section is that element itself. The section changes its combinatorial type only where the hyperplane passes through vertices. Between these levels its volume is a polynomial in $t$ of degree at most $3$, and its integral over $t$ is the four-dimensional volume of the polytope.

Penrose tilings

The tilings of the plane above are periodic: they are mapped to themselves by translations in two independent directions. Penrose found pairs of tiles that tile the plane, but only non-periodically, when their edges are matched by a rule [@penrose1974]: a thick rhomb, with angles $72^\circ$ and $108^\circ$, and a thin one, with angles $36^\circ$ and $144^\circ$; or a kite and a dart. Arcs of two colours are drawn on the tiles, and the rule is that where two tiles share an edge, the arcs meeting it on either side have the same colour. The rule can be obeyed over the whole plane, but no tiling that obeys it is periodic.

Each tile can be cut into tiles and halves of tiles $\varphi = \tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2$ times smaller, and the halves from neighbouring tiles join up, so that the pieces of all the tiles make up another Penrose tiling. Conversely, the tiles of any Penrose tiling group into patches, one for each tile of a Penrose tiling with larger tiles, and the larger tiling is determined by the smaller one. In a large disc of a Penrose tiling, the number of thick rhombs divided by the number of thin ones is close to $\varphi$, and so is the number of kites divided by the number of darts [@grunbaum1987, Ch. 10]. The rhomb tilings can also be made by projection: the points of the lattice $\ZZ^5$ that lie close to a suitable plane in $\RR^5$, projected to that plane, are the vertices of a Penrose rhomb tiling [@debruijn1981].
