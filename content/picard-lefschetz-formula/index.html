The function $f=p(x)+y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2$, for a polynomial $p$ of degree $\mu+1$ with distinct critical values $z_1,\dots,z_\mu$, is a Morse perturbation of the singularity $x^{\mu+1}+y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2$. Its level set $V_z=f^{-1}(z)$ has a vanishing cycle $\Delta_i$ for each critical value, a sphere that shrinks to a point as $z$ goes to $z_i$ along a path. Taking $z$ once round $z_i$ changes each cycle $a$ of $V_z$ by the Picard–Lefschetz formula, $h_i(a)=a+(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\langle a,\Delta_i\rangle\,\Delta_i$.

Vanishing cycles

Let $p$ be a monic polynomial of degree $\mu+1$ whose critical points $c_1,\dots,c_\mu$ are simple and whose critical values $z_i=p(c_i)$ are distinct. The critical points of $f=p(x)+y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2$ are the points $(c_i,0,\dots,0)$, all nondegenerate, so the level set $V_z=f^{-1}(z)$ is a smooth complex manifold for every $z$ other than $z_1,\dots,z_\mu$. It is homotopy equivalent to a bouquet of $\mu$ spheres $S^{n-1}$, as Milnor showed for the level sets near any isolated critical point [@milnor1968], so that

$$H_{n-1}(V_z)\cong\ZZ^\mu$$

(in reduced homology for $n=1$, where $V_z$ is $\mu+1$ points).

Near $c_i$ there are coordinates in which $f=z_i+x^2+y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2$. For $z$ near $z_i$ the level set there is the quadric $x^2+y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2=z-z_i$, which contains the sphere of its points in $\sqrt{z-z_i}\,\RR^n$, and this sphere shrinks to the point $c_i$ as $z$ tends to $z_i$, where the quadric becomes a cone. Carried back along a path $\gamma$ from a regular value to $z_i$, it gives a class in the homology of the level set at the start of $\gamma$, the vanishing cycle along $\gamma$, defined up to its orientation [@arnold1988, Ch. 1].

Since $f$ is $p(x)$ plus a sum of squares, its vanishing cycles can be seen in the plane of $x$. Over a point $x$ the level set is the quadric $y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2=z-p(x)$, which contains the sphere $\sqrt{z-p(x)}\,S^{n-2}$ and degenerates to a point where $p(x)=z$. A path from $z$ to $z_i$ lifts through $c_i$ to an arc between two roots of $p(x)=z$, and the vanishing cycle is made of the spheres over the points of the arc [@seidel2008]. For the straight path from $z$ to $z_i$ the arc is the line through $c_i$ on which $p(x)-z$ has the phase of $z_i-z$, so in the picture of $x$ it runs along the colour of $c_i$'s phase.

The straight paths from $z$ to the critical values meet only at $z$, and numbered in the order in which they leave it, clockwise, they form a distinguished system of paths; their vanishing cycles $\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_\mu$ form a basis of $H_{n-1}(V_z)$ [@arnold1988].

The Picard–Lefschetz formula

Let $\tau_i$ be the loop that goes from $z$ along the straight path to near $z_i$, once round $z_i$ counterclockwise, and back. Carrying the cycles of $V_z$ along $\tau_i$ gives an automorphism $h_i$ of $H_{n-1}(V_z)$, and the Picard–Lefschetz formula says that

$$h_i(a)=a+(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\,\langle a,\Delta_i\rangle\,\Delta_i,\tag{1}$$

where $\langle\ ,\ \rangle$ is the intersection form of $V_z$, defined with its complex orientation [@arnold1988, Ch. 1]. It is named after Picard, who studied the cycles on the curves of a family of plane curves [@picard1897], and Lefschetz, who studied them in every dimension [@lefschetz1924].

The self-intersection of a vanishing cycle is

$$\langle\Delta,\Delta\rangle=\begin{cases}(-1)^{(n-1)(n-2)/2}\cdot2,&n\text{ odd},\\0,&n\text{ even}.\end{cases}$$

So for odd $n$, $h_i(\Delta_i)=-\Delta_i$ and $h_i$ is the reflection in the hyperplane orthogonal to $\Delta_i$. For even $n$, $h_i(\Delta_i)=\Delta_i$ and $h_i$ is a transvection; for $n=2$ it is the action of the Dehn twist of the curve $V_z$ about the circle $\Delta_i$.

Only the part of $V_z$ near $c_i$ changes as $z$ goes round $z_i$. There the two roots at the ends of $\Delta_i$ are $c_i\pm\sqrt{(z-z_i)/a_i}$ to first order, with $a_i=\frac12p''(c_i)$, so they turn half a turn round each other, as in the figure. An arc that ends at one of them comes back as the arc that runs along it and then along $\Delta_i$: it has become $a\pm\Delta_i$, with the sign of $(1)$.

The basis of straight paths in the picture at the top does not change as $z$ goes round $z_i$: it is the same basis when $z$ is back. Instead it jumps where $z$ crosses a Stokes ray, the ray from $z_k$ away from $z_j$: there the straight path to $z_j$ runs through $z_k$, $\Delta_j$ runs into $c_k$, and across the ray the path to $z_j$ changes by the loop $\tau_k$, so that $\Delta_j$ becomes $h_k^{\pm1}(\Delta_j)$. This is the Stokes phenomenon of Stokes phenomenon, where the thimbles jump in the same way.

The intersection form

The arcs of a distinguished basis meet only at their ends. Two vanishing cycles whose arcs share an end meet once, at the point over it, and $\langle\Delta_i,\Delta_j\rangle=\pm1$; cycles over disjoint arcs do not meet. The form is symmetric for odd $n$ and skew-symmetric for even $n$, and adding two squares changes its sign without changing the monodromy [@arnold1988]:

$$\langle a,b\rangle_{n+2}=-\langle a,b\rangle_n,\qquad h^{(n+2)}_i=h^{(n)}_i.$$

For $n=3$, $-\langle\ ,\ \rangle$ is positive definite, and the vanishing cycles are roots of the root system $\mathrm{A}_\mu$ (see Root systems); the basis the page opens with is a chain, with the Dynkin diagram of $\mathrm{A}_\mu$. Where $z$ crosses a Stokes ray the basis changes, and so does the diagram, though the lattice it describes does not.

Monodromy

A loop that $z$ travels is a product of the loops $\tau_i$ and their inverses, which generate the fundamental group of the plane without the critical values, a free group. The monodromy of the loop is the automorphism $h$ it gives; if it is $\tau_{i_1}^{\pm1}$, then $\tau_{i_2}^{\pm1}$, and so on, then $h=\cdots h_{i_2}^{\pm1}h_{i_1}^{\pm1}$, which preserves the intersection form. Next to the picture at the top, the cycles taken at $z_0$ (where the critical points were last moved) are written, carried along with $z$, in the basis at $z$: back at $z_0$ they show the monodromy of the loop $z$ has travelled.

The monodromy group is generated by $h_1,\dots,h_\mu$. For odd $n$ these are reflections, and they generate the Weyl group of $\mathrm{A}_\mu$, the symmetric group on $\mu+1$ letters; for $n=1$ it permutes the $\mu+1$ points of $V_z$. For $n=2$ the group is infinite: for $\mu=2$, $V_z$ is a torus with a hole and the group is $\mathrm{SL}_2(\ZZ)$.

Once round all the critical values is the classical monodromy, $h=h_1h_2\cdots h_\mu$ for a distinguished basis [@arnold1988]. For $f=x^{\mu+1}+y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2$ its eigenvalues are $(-1)^{n-1}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k/(\mu+1)}$ for $k=1,\dots,\mu$; in the basis the page opens with it takes $\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_{\mu-1}$ to $\pm\Delta_2,\dots,\pm\Delta_\mu$.
