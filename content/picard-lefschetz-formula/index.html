For a function $f\colon X\to\CC$ with isolated nondegenerate critical points, Picard–Lefschetz theory describes how its fibres $X_a = f^{-1}(a)$ change as $a$ goes round a critical value $a_i$ of $f$. Each fibre has a vanishing cycle $\Delta_i \subset X_a$ for each critical value $a_i$, an $(n-1)$-sphere that shrinks to a point as $a$ goes to $a_i$, where $n = \dim X$. Taking $a$ once round $a_i$ changes each cycle $\gamma$ of $X_a$ by the Picard–Lefschetz formula, $h_i(\gamma)=\gamma+(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\langle\gamma,\Delta_i\rangle\,\Delta_i$.

Vanishing cycles

Consider a holomorphic function $f\colon X\to\CC$ on a complex manifold of dimension $n$ whose critical points are isolated and nondegenerate, with distinct critical values, and which has no critical points at infinity. Near a critical point with critical value $a_i$, $f$ is a sum of squares

$$f (z) - a_i = z_1^2 + \cdots + z_n^2$$

for suitable coordinates $z_1,\dots,z_n$ round it, and for $a = a_i + \varepsilon$ with small $|\varepsilon| > 0$, the nearby fibre $X_a = f^{-1} (a)$ is, near the critical point,

$$\{ z_1^2 + \cdots + z_n^2 = \varepsilon \},$$

and contains a sphere $S^{n-1}$, the vanishing cycle, where all $z_k / \sqrt{\varepsilon} \in \RR$, which shrinks to a point as $\varepsilon \to 0$. Strictly speaking, the vanishing cycle is the class

$$\Delta_i \in H_{n-1}(X_a)$$

represented by the sphere $S^{n-1}$, well-defined up to its orientation (in reduced homology when $n = 1$). Picard–Lefschetz theory describes what happens to cycles when $a$ goes round a critical value $a_i$, which only depends on this local form.

Here, we follow one example in which all of this can be seen in a plane. Take $X=\CC^n$, and consider the function

$$f (x, y_2, \dots, y_n) = p(x) + y_2^2 + \cdots + y_n^2,$$

where $p$ is a monic polynomial of degree $d+1$ with simple critical points $x_1,\dots,x_d$ and distinct critical values $a_i=p(x_i)$. The critical points of $f$ are the points $(x_i,0,\dots,0)$, and the level set $X_a=f^{-1}(a)$ is a smooth complex manifold for every $a$ other than $a_1,\dots,a_d$. It is homotopy equivalent to a bouquet of $d$ spheres $S^{n-1}$, as can be seen from the picture at the top, so that

$$H_{n-1}(X_a)\simeq\ZZ^d$$

(in reduced homology for $n=1$, where $X_a$ is $d+1$ points), and it has a basis given by the vanishing cycles $\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_d$, each obtained by moving $a$ towards $a_i$ along a straight path, as long as it does not pass through any other $a_j$.

The Picard–Lefschetz formula

Let $\tau_i$ be the loop that goes from $a$ along the straight path to near $a_i$, once round $a_i$ counterclockwise, and back. Carrying the cycles of $X_a$ along $\tau_i$ gives an automorphism $h_i$ of $H_{n-1}(X_a)$, called the monodromy, and it is given by the Picard–Lefschetz formula

$$h_i(\gamma)=\gamma+(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\,\langle\gamma,\Delta_i\rangle\,\Delta_i,\tag{1}$$

where $\langle\ ,\ \rangle$ is the intersection form of $X_a$, defined with its complex orientation [@arnold1988, Ch. 1]. The picture at the top shows this: take $a$ once round $a_i$, by hand or from the bar, and the cycles $\Delta_j$ that meet $\Delta_i$ come back running along it.

The self-intersection of a vanishing cycle is

$$\langle\Delta,\Delta\rangle=\begin{cases}(-1)^{(n-1)(n-2)/2}\cdot2,&n\text{ odd},\\0,&n\text{ even}.\end{cases}$$

So for odd $n$, $h_i(\Delta_i)=-\Delta_i$ and $h_i$ is the reflection in the hyperplane orthogonal to $\Delta_i$. For even $n$, $h_i(\Delta_i)=\Delta_i$ and $h_i$ is a transvection; for $n=2$ it is the action of the Dehn twist of the curve $X_a$ about the circle $\Delta_i$.

On the other hand, the basis $\Delta_i$ given by straight paths in the picture at the top does not change after $a$ goes round $a_i$: it is the same basis when $a$ is back (choose Show $\Delta_i$ to see it). Instead it jumps where $a$ crosses a Stokes ray, the ray from $a_k$ away from $a_j$: there the straight path to $a_j$ runs through $a_k$, $\Delta_j$ runs into $x_k$, and across the ray the path to $a_j$ changes by the loop $\tau_k$, so that $\Delta_j$ becomes $h_k^{\pm1}(\Delta_j)$. The thimbles of Stokes phenomenon jump in the same way.

The intersection form

The arcs of a basis $\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_d$ meet only at their ends. Two vanishing cycles whose arcs share an end meet once, at the point over it, and $\langle\Delta_i,\Delta_j\rangle=\pm1$; cycles over disjoint arcs do not meet. The form is symmetric for odd $n$ and skew-symmetric for even $n$, and increasing $n$ by $2$ changes its sign without changing the monodromy [@arnold1988]:

$$\langle\gamma,\delta\rangle_{n+2}=-\langle\gamma,\delta\rangle_n,\qquad h^{(n+2)}_i=h^{(n)}_i.$$

For $n=3$, $-\langle\ ,\ \rangle$ is positive definite, and the vanishing cycles are roots of the root system $\mathrm{A}_d$ (see Root systems); the basis the page opens with is a chain, with the Dynkin diagram of $\mathrm{A}_d$. Where $a$ crosses a Stokes ray the basis changes, and so does the diagram, though the lattice it describes does not.

Monodromy

When $a$ travels along a loop which is a product of the loops $\tau_i$ and their inverses, the monodromy of the loop is the automorphism $h$ which is the product of the corresponding operators $h_i^{\pm 1}$, which preserves the intersection form. The fundamental group of the plane without the critical values $a_i$ is a free group generated by the loops $\tau_i$, so these loops give all the possible monodromies.

The monodromy group is the group generated by $h_1,\dots,h_d$. For odd $n$ these are reflections, and they generate the Weyl group of $\mathrm{A}_d$, the symmetric group on $d+1$ letters; for $n=1$ it permutes the $d+1$ points of $X_a$. For $n=2$ the group is infinite: for $d=2$, $X_a$ is a punctured torus and the group is $\mathrm{SL}_2(\ZZ)$.

The monodromy for the loop that goes once round all the critical values is the classical monodromy $h=h_1h_2\cdots h_d$ [@arnold1988]. That loop can be widened to a large circle, far from all the critical values, so up to conjugation $h$ stays the same when the critical points move, and its eigenvalues do not change. Bringing the critical points together at $0$ gives $f=x^{d+1}+y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2$, whose monodromy has eigenvalues $(-1)^{n-1}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k/(d+1)}$ for $k=1,\dots,d$; in the basis the page opens with it takes $\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_{d-1}$ to $\pm\Delta_2,\dots,\pm\Delta_d$.
