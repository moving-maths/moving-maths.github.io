For a function $f\colon X\to\CC$ with isolated nondegenerate critical points, Picard–Lefschetz theory describes how its fibres $X_a = f^{-1}(a)$ change as $a$ goes round a critical value $a_i$ of $f$. Each fibre has a vanishing cycle $\Delta_i \subset X_a$ for each critical value $a_i$, an $(n-1)$-sphere that shrinks to a point as $a$ goes to $a_i$, where $n = \dim X$. Taking $a$ once round $a_i$ changes each cycle $\gamma$ of $X_a$ by the Picard–Lefschetz formula, $h_i(\gamma)=\gamma+(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\langle\gamma,\Delta_i\rangle\,\Delta_i$.

Vanishing cycles

In general, $f\colon X\to\CC$ is a holomorphic function on a complex manifold of dimension $n$ whose critical points are isolated and nondegenerate, with distinct critical values, and which is well behaved at infinity (proper, for instance); Lefschetz studied the fibres of a pencil of hyperplane sections of a projective variety in this way [@lefschetz1924]. Over the regular values its fibres form a fibre bundle, so a loop of values carries the homology of a fibre back to itself. Near each critical point $f$ is a sum of squares in suitable coordinates, which gives a vanishing cycle, and what a loop round a single critical value does depends only on this local form [@arnold1988]. This page follows one example, on $X=\CC^n$, in which all of this can be seen in the plane of $x$.

Let $p$ be a monic polynomial of degree $d+1$ whose critical points $x_1,\dots,x_d$ are simple and whose critical values $a_i=p(x_i)$ are distinct. The critical points of $f=p(x)+y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2$ are the points $(x_i,0,\dots,0)$, all nondegenerate, so the level set $X_a=f^{-1}(a)$ is a smooth complex manifold for every $a$ other than $a_1,\dots,a_d$. It is homotopy equivalent to a bouquet of $d$ spheres $S^{n-1}$, as Milnor showed for the level sets near any isolated critical point [@milnor1968], so that

$$H_{n-1}(X_a)\cong\ZZ^d$$

(in reduced homology for $n=1$, where $X_a$ is $d+1$ points).

Near $x_i$ there are coordinates in which $f=a_i+x^2+y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2$. For $a$ near $a_i$ the level set there is the quadric $x^2+y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2=a-a_i$, which contains the sphere of its points in $\sqrt{a-a_i}\,\RR^n$, and this sphere shrinks to the point $x_i$ as $a$ tends to $a_i$, where the quadric becomes a cone. Carried back along a path $\ell$ from a regular value to $a_i$, it gives a class in the homology of the level set at the start of $\ell$, the vanishing cycle along $\ell$, defined up to its orientation [@arnold1988, Ch. 1].

Since $f$ is $p(x)$ plus a sum of squares, its vanishing cycles can be seen in the plane of $x$. Over a point $x$ the level set is the quadric $y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2=a-p(x)$, which contains the sphere $\sqrt{a-p(x)}\,S^{n-2}$ and degenerates to a point where $p(x)=a$. A path from $a$ to $a_i$ lifts through $x_i$ to an arc between two roots of $p(x)=a$, and the vanishing cycle is made of the spheres over the points of the arc [@seidel2008]. For the straight path from $a$ to $a_i$ the arc is the line through $x_i$ on which $p(x)-a$ has the phase of $a_i-a$: it runs from the saddle $x_i$ of $|p(x)-a|$ down to two roots, the way water runs, across the level lines in the picture at the top (with Phase in the bar, it runs along the colour of $x_i$).

The straight paths from $a$ to the critical values meet only at $a$, and numbered in the order in which they leave it, clockwise, they form a distinguished system of paths; their vanishing cycles $\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_d$ form a basis of $H_{n-1}(X_a)$ [@arnold1988].

The Picard–Lefschetz formula

Let $\tau_i$ be the loop that goes from $a$ along the straight path to near $a_i$, once round $a_i$ counterclockwise, and back. Carrying the cycles of $X_a$ along $\tau_i$ gives an automorphism $h_i$ of $H_{n-1}(X_a)$, and the Picard–Lefschetz formula says that

$$h_i(\gamma)=\gamma+(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\,\langle\gamma,\Delta_i\rangle\,\Delta_i,\tag{1}$$

where $\langle\ ,\ \rangle$ is the intersection form of $X_a$, defined with its complex orientation [@arnold1988, Ch. 1]. It is named after Picard, who studied the cycles on the curves of a family of plane curves [@picard1897], and Lefschetz, who studied them in every dimension [@lefschetz1924].

The self-intersection of a vanishing cycle is

$$\langle\Delta,\Delta\rangle=\begin{cases}(-1)^{(n-1)(n-2)/2}\cdot2,&n\text{ odd},\\0,&n\text{ even}.\end{cases}$$

So for odd $n$, $h_i(\Delta_i)=-\Delta_i$ and $h_i$ is the reflection in the hyperplane orthogonal to $\Delta_i$. For even $n$, $h_i(\Delta_i)=\Delta_i$ and $h_i$ is a transvection; for $n=2$ it is the action of the Dehn twist of the curve $X_a$ about the circle $\Delta_i$.

Only the part of $X_a$ near $x_i$ changes as $a$ goes round $a_i$. There the two roots at the ends of $\Delta_i$ are $x_i\pm\sqrt{(a-a_i)/b_i}$ to first order, with $b_i=\frac12p''(x_i)$, so they turn half a turn round each other. An arc that ends at one of them comes back as the arc that runs along it and then along $\Delta_i$: it has become $\gamma\pm\Delta_i$, with the sign of $(1)$. The picture at the top shows this: take $a$ once round $a_i$, by hand or from the bar, and the cycles that meet $\Delta_i$ come back running along it.

The basis of straight paths in the picture at the top does not change as $a$ goes round $a_i$: it is the same basis when $a$ is back (choose $\Delta_i$ in the bar to see it). Instead it jumps where $a$ crosses a Stokes ray, the ray from $a_k$ away from $a_j$: there the straight path to $a_j$ runs through $a_k$, $\Delta_j$ runs into $x_k$, and across the ray the path to $a_j$ changes by the loop $\tau_k$, so that $\Delta_j$ becomes $h_k^{\pm1}(\Delta_j)$. This is the Stokes phenomenon of Stokes phenomenon, where the thimbles jump in the same way.

The intersection form

The arcs of a distinguished basis meet only at their ends. Two vanishing cycles whose arcs share an end meet once, at the point over it, and $\langle\Delta_i,\Delta_j\rangle=\pm1$; cycles over disjoint arcs do not meet. The form is symmetric for odd $n$ and skew-symmetric for even $n$, and adding two squares changes its sign without changing the monodromy [@arnold1988]:

$$\langle\gamma,\delta\rangle_{n+2}=-\langle\gamma,\delta\rangle_n,\qquad h^{(n+2)}_i=h^{(n)}_i.$$

For $n=3$, $-\langle\ ,\ \rangle$ is positive definite, and the vanishing cycles are roots of the root system $\mathrm{A}_d$ (see Root systems); the basis the page opens with is a chain, with the Dynkin diagram of $\mathrm{A}_d$. Where $a$ crosses a Stokes ray the basis changes, and so does the diagram, though the lattice it describes does not.

Monodromy

A loop that $a$ travels is a product of the loops $\tau_i$ and their inverses, which generate the fundamental group of the plane without the critical values, a free group. The monodromy of the loop is the automorphism $h$ it gives; if it is $\tau_{i_1}^{\pm1}$, then $\tau_{i_2}^{\pm1}$, and so on, then $h=\cdots h_{i_2}^{\pm1}h_{i_1}^{\pm1}$, which preserves the intersection form. Next to the picture at the top, $h(\Delta_i)$ is $\Delta_i$ carried along the path of $a$ since it was picked up (or along the loop chosen in the bar), written in the basis at $a$, with the path closed up by the straight segment back to its start. The picture shows these cycles (with $\Delta_i$ in the bar, the basis instead): their arcs are dragged along with the roots and pulled tight once $a$ rests, a number beside an arc saying how many of its strands run together there, and while $h$ is the identity they are drawn as the basis. For odd $n$ the class of a sphere over an arc depends only on the roots at its ends, as for $n=1$, so that $h_i^2=1$ though the spheres carried wind once more round the ends of $\Delta_i$ each time $a$ goes round $a_i$; there each $h(\Delta_i)$ is drawn along the arcs of the basis that join its ends.

The monodromy group is generated by $h_1,\dots,h_d$. For odd $n$ these are reflections, and they generate the Weyl group of $\mathrm{A}_d$, the symmetric group on $d+1$ letters; for $n=1$ it permutes the $d+1$ points of $X_a$. For $n=2$ the group is infinite: for $d=2$, $X_a$ is a torus with a hole and the group is $\mathrm{SL}_2(\ZZ)$.

Once round all the critical values is the classical monodromy, $h=h_1h_2\cdots h_d$ for a distinguished basis [@arnold1988]. For $f=x^{d+1}+y_2^2+\cdots+y_n^2$ its eigenvalues are $(-1)^{n-1}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k/(d+1)}$ for $k=1,\dots,d$; in the basis the page opens with it takes $\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_{d-1}$ to $\pm\Delta_2,\dots,\pm\Delta_d$.
