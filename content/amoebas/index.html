The amoeba of a curve $f(z,w)=0$ in $(\CC^\times)^2$ is its image under $(z,w)\mapsto(\log|z|,\log|w|)$. As the coefficients of $f$ spread apart in size, it limits to a tropical curve, which is dual to a subdivision of the Newton polygon of $f$.

The curve and its amoeba

Consider the curve in $(\CC^\times)^2$ defined by

$$f(z,w)=\sum_{(i,j)\in I}a_{ij}\,z^iw^j=0,\qquad a_{ij}=\pm t^{v_{ij}},$$

where $I\subset\ZZ^2$ is a finite set, $t>1$, and the $v_{ij}$ are real parameters, called the height of $(i,j)$. The Newton polygon $\Delta$ of $f$ is the convex hull of $I$ in $\RR^2$. For the curve of degree $d$ that the page opens with, its Newton polygon is the triangle with corners $(0,0)$, $(d,0)$ and $(0,d)$. Adding or deleting terms of $f$ can change its Newton polygon.

The amoeba of the curve is its image under $(z,w)\mapsto(\log|z|,\log|w|)$ [@gelfand1994]. The figure shows it scaled by $1/\log t$, in the coordinates

$$x=\log_t|z|,\qquad y=\log_t|w|.$$

The tropical limit

The tropical curve $T$ of the heights is the set of points $(x,y)$ at which the maximum

$$\max_{(i,j)\in I}\,\bigl(v_{ij}+ix+jy\bigr)$$

is attained at least twice. Over a point $(x,y)$ the terms of $f$ have sizes $|a_{ij}z^iw^j|=t^{\,v_{ij}+ix+jy}$, so in each region of the complement of $T$ one term is the largest, and the region is named after its monomial. Where one term is larger than all the others together, $f$ has no zeros. So the amoeba lies in the band where the two largest of the numbers ${v_{ij}+ix+jy}$ differ by at most $\log_t(n-1)$, $n$ being the number of terms, and the band narrows onto $T$ as $t$ grows [@mikhalkin2004; @maclagan2015, §1.4].

The roof in the figure of the Newton polygon is the upper convex hull of the lifted points $(i,j,v_{ij})$. Its faces project to a subdivision of the Newton polygon, dual to $T$: each face to a vertex of $T$, where the terms of the face are the largest and equal; each inner edge to an edge of $T$ at right angles to it, whose weight is the lattice length of the inner edge; each edge on the boundary to a ray. The curve $T$ is smooth when the faces are triangles of area $\tfrac12$. A lattice point under the roof, or on it but not at a corner, gives a term that is never alone the largest. An interior point at a corner gives a cycle of $T$, and so, once $t$ is large, a hole of the amoeba.

Area

In the coordinates $(\log|z|,\log|w|)$ the area of an amoeba is at most $\pi^2$ times the area of its Newton polygon [@passare2004]:

$$\operatorname{Area}\le\pi^2\operatorname{Area}(\Delta),$$

which is $\tfrac{d^2}{2}\pi^2$ for the curve of degree $d$ that the page opens with. It equals this bound exactly when the curve, after $z$ and $w$ are multiplied by constants, is a simple Harnack curve [@mikhalkin2001]. The real points of a simple Harnack curve trace the boundary of its amoeba [@mikhalkin2000]. In the chart, with the signs minus where $i$ and $j$ are both even and a smooth tropical curve, the area comes up to the bound as $t$ grows.
