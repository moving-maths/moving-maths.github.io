A Dynkin diagram is a picture of a Cartan matrix. Its type, finite, affine, hyperbolic or indefinite, is decided by a symmetric form. For the roots themselves, in rank two and three, see Root systems.

The Cartan matrix

A Cartan matrix is a square matrix $A=(a_{ij})$ of integers with $a_{ii}=2$, with $a_{ij}\le0$ for $i\ne j$, and with $a_{ij}=0$ exactly when $a_{ji}=0$ (a generalized Cartan matrix in [@kac1990, Ch. 1]). The simple roots $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n$ of a root system give a Cartan matrix, with entries

$$a_{ij}=\frac{2(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)}{(\alpha_i,\alpha_i)}.$$

The Dynkin diagram of a Cartan matrix has a node for each $i$. Nodes $i$ and $j$ are joined when $a_{ij}\ne0$, by $\max(\lvert a_{ij}\rvert,\lvert a_{ji}\rvert)$ lines, with an arrow pointing to node $i$ when $\lvert a_{ij}\rvert>1$; for a root system, the arrow points to the shorter root. When $a_{ij}a_{ji}>4$ they are joined instead by one thick line labelled $\lvert a_{ij}\rvert,\lvert a_{ji}\rvert$ [@kac1990, Ch. 4]. So a single line is $a_{ij}=a_{ji}=-1$, and a double line with its arrow pointing to node $i$ is $a_{ij}=-2$ and $a_{ji}=-1$.

A Coxeter diagram may also have bonds labelled $m$, which stand for $a_{ij}=a_{ji}=-2\cos(\pi/m)$ [@humphreys1990, Ch. 5]. The reflections $s_i(\alpha_j)=\alpha_j-a_{ij}\alpha_i$ generate the Weyl group of a Cartan matrix [@kac1990, Ch. 3], or the Coxeter group of a Coxeter diagram.

Finite, affine and indefinite

The eigenvalues of $A$ decide the type of a connected diagram. It is of finite type when they are all positive, of affine type when one is $0$ and the others are positive, and of indefinite type otherwise [@kac1990, Ch. 4; @humphreys1990, Ch. 6].

This holds when $A$ is symmetrizable, that is, when there are positive numbers $d_i$ with $d_ia_{ij}=d_ja_{ji}$. Then $(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)=d_ia_{ij}$ is a symmetric bilinear form on the span of the simple roots, the eigenvalues of $A$ are real, and their signs give the signature of the form. When a matrix is not symmetrizable, its eigenvalues need not be real, and it is of indefinite type.

For a diagram of affine type the kernel of the form is spanned by $\delta=\sum_ic_i\alpha_i$, with positive integers $c_i$, the numbers on the nodes. A diagram of indefinite type is hyperbolic when every proper connected subdiagram is of finite or affine type [@kac1990, Ch. 5].

The Coxeter plane

The figure needs a connected diagram of finite type, of rank at least $2$.

For a diagram of finite type the Weyl or Coxeter group $W$ is finite. A Coxeter element $c=s_1s_2\cdots s_n$, the product of the simple reflections in any order, has order $h$, the Coxeter number, and its eigenvalues are

$$\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\,m_1/h},\ \dots,\ \mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\,m_n/h},$$

where $m_1\le\dots\le m_n$ are the exponents [@humphreys1990, Ch. 3]. The Coxeter plane is the plane on which $c$ acts by the rotation through $2\pi/h$. The $nh$ roots fall into $n$ orbits of $c$, each of $h$ roots, and each orbit projects to the plane as a regular $h$-gon [@humphreys1990, Ch. 3]. Each pair of exponents $m$ and $h-m$ gives a plane on which $c$ turns by $2\pi m/h$; from rank $3$ on, the figure projects the roots to one of these planes, and for depth to one more direction.

Heights and exponents

With a bond labelled $m$, some roots have coefficients that are not integers, such as $2\cos(\pi/m)$, and the roots have no heights.

A positive root $\beta=\sum_ic_i\alpha_i$ has height $\sum_ic_i$. For a diagram of finite type the numbers of positive roots of heights $1,2,3,\dots$ form a partition, and the conjugate partition is formed by the exponents [@kostant1959]. So there are $n$ roots of height $1$, the simple roots, and the highest root has height $m_n=h-1$.

For a diagram of affine type a real root plus $r\delta$ is again a real root, where $r$ is the $r$ of the name $\mathrm{X}_\ell^{(r)}$, so the numbers repeat. For affine and indefinite types there are infinitely many real roots, and the figure counts them up to height $30$.
