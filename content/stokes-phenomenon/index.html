An integral $I(\hbar)=\int_\Gamma \mathrm{e}^{-S(x)/\hbar}\,dx$ of a polynomial $S$, along a path $\Gamma$ running to infinity where the integrand decays, is a sum of integrals over Lefschetz thimbles, the paths of steepest descent from the critical points of $S$. Each has a divergent asymptotic series in $\hbar$. As the phase of $\hbar$ turns, the thimbles jump, and the asymptotic expansion of $I(\hbar)$ with them: the Stokes phenomenon. The jumps and the series of the other critical points determine the large orders of each series: resurgence.

Thimbles

Let $S$ be a polynomial of degree $n+1$, with critical points $p_1,\dots,p_n$, and let $\hbar\ne0$ be complex. The integrand $\mathrm{e}^{-S/\hbar}$ dies in the $n+1$ sectors at infinity where $\mathrm{Re}(S/\hbar)\to+\infty$, the valleys, marked on the rim of the picture at the top. The integral converges along a path $\Gamma$ that comes in along one valley and goes out along another, and by Cauchy's theorem it depends only on the two valleys. The thimble $J_i$ of $p_i$ is the path through it on which $(S(x)-S(p_i))/\hbar$ is real and positive: along it the phase of the integrand is constant and its modulus falls as steeply as it can. The map $S$ takes it two to one onto the ray $S(p_i)+\hbar\,[0,\infty)$, drawn in the plane of values [@witten2011].

The upward flow of $\mathrm{Re}(S/\hbar)$,

$$\frac{dx}{dt}=\overline{S'(x)/\hbar},$$

keeps $\mathrm{Im}(S/\hbar)$ constant, and Flow moves $\Gamma$ along it. It carries every point of $\Gamma$ out to a valley, except the points on the ascending paths $K_i$, on which $(S(x)-S(p_i))/\hbar$ is real and negative; these run into the critical points. So the flow presses $\Gamma$ onto the thimbles,

$$\Gamma=\sum_i n_i J_i,$$

where $n_i$ is the number of times $\Gamma$ crosses $K_i$, counted with signs [@witten2011]. The same flow deforms the domains of lattice path integrals towards thimbles, to tame their oscillation [@alexandru2022].

The Stokes phenomenon

Each thimble integral has an asymptotic expansion as $\hbar\to0$ in a fixed direction, found by expanding $S$ about $p_i$:

$$\int_{J_i}\mathrm{e}^{-S/\hbar}\,dx\;\sim\;\mathrm{e}^{-S(p_i)/\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar}{S''(p_i)}}\;\sum_{k\ge0}a^{(i)}_k\hbar^k,\qquad a^{(i)}_0=1.$$

So $I(\hbar)$ is a sum of exponentials, one for each critical point with $n_i\ne0$, each with its series.

The thimble $J_i$ jumps when its ray runs into another critical value $S(p_j)$, at $\arg\hbar=\arg\bigl(S(p_j)-S(p_i)\bigr)$. There $J_i$ runs into $p_j$, and as $\arg\hbar$ grows, $J_i\mapsto J_i\pm J_j$: the Picard–Lefschetz formula [@pham1983]. The integral does not jump, so the coefficients do: $n_j$ changes by $\mp n_i$, and a term $\mathrm{e}^{-S(p_j)/\hbar}$ enters the expansion of $I(\hbar)$ or leaves it. This is the Stokes phenomenon, which Stokes found in Airy's integral [@stokes1864]. On the Stokes ray the term that jumps is as small as it can be beside $\mathrm{e}^{-S(p_i)/\hbar}$, about the size of the smallest term of its series, which cannot resolve it. It becomes visible where the two exponentials have the same size, on the anti-Stokes lines, where their curves in the chart cross.

The picture of $I(\hbar)$ beside the plane of $x$ shows both sides of this. The integral is analytic in $\hbar\ne0$, and its colours run on across the Stokes rays where its expansion jumps. Past a Stokes ray, where the term that has jumped in grows to the size of the other, the two cancel at points without end, accumulating at $\hbar=0$: the zeros of $I$, dark in the picture, like the zeros of Airy's function on the negative axis.

After a whole turn of $\hbar$ the valleys have each moved on by one, and $\Gamma$ with them: $I(\hbar)$ is not a single-valued function of $\hbar$, and the coefficients after a turn are those of another path. The picture of $I(\hbar)$ takes it from $\hbar$ the short way round, so it is cut along the ray opposite $\hbar$.

The Borel plane

The Borel transform of the series of $J_i$,

$$\beta_i(\zeta)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{a^{(i)}_k}{(\frac12)_k}\,\zeta^k,\qquad(\tfrac12)_k=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac32\cdots\bigl(k-\tfrac12\bigr),$$

converges near $0$, and substituting $\zeta=S(x)-S(p_i)$ in the integral over the thimble gives it back as a Laplace integral along the ray of $\hbar$:

$$\int_{J_i}\mathrm{e}^{-S/\hbar}\,dx=\mathrm{e}^{-S(p_i)/\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{2}{S''(p_i)}}\int_0^{\infty\cdot\hbar}\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta/\hbar}\,\beta_i(\zeta)\,\frac{d\zeta}{\sqrt\zeta}.$$

The Borel plane is the plane of values of $S$ with $S(p_i)$ moved to $0$, and the integrand decays along the ray over the length $|\hbar|$. Along each ray from $0$, $\beta_i$ continues analytically until the ray meets one of the points $A_{ij}=S(p_j)-S(p_i)$ for which $J_i$ runs into $p_j$ at $\arg\hbar=\arg A_{ij}$. There it has a singularity like $(\zeta-A_{ij})^{-1/2}$, and beyond it the values on the two sides of the ray differ. So the Laplace integral jumps when the ray of $\hbar$ crosses a singularity, exactly as the thimble does [@ecalle1981; @aniceto2019]. The other critical values are singularities only on other sheets.

Resurgence

With $S(p_i+y)-S(p_i)=s_2y^2\bigl(1+T(y)\bigr)$, substituting $w^2=S(x)-S(p_i)$ in the integral and inverting the series gives

$$a^{(i)}_k=\frac{(2k-1)!!}{(2s_2)^k}\,[y^{2k}]\,\bigl(1+T(y)\bigr)^{-(2k+1)/2}.$$

These are, up to $(\frac12)_k$, the Taylor coefficients of $\beta_i$, so they grow like $(k-1)!/|A|^k$ with $A$ the nearest singularity: the series diverges for every $\hbar\ne0$. Its terms first fall and then grow; summed up to the smallest, near $k\approx|A/\hbar|$, it comes as close to the integral as it can, within about $\mathrm{e}^{-|A/\hbar|}$, which is the size of the jumping exponential on the Stokes ray [@boyd1999].

Across a Stokes ray of $J_i$ the Laplace integrals of $\beta_i$ on its two sides differ by the jump of the thimble: the sum of the series of $p_j$ times $\kappa_{ij}\,\mathrm{e}^{-A_{ij}/\hbar}$, where the Stokes constant $\kappa_{ij}=\pm\sqrt{S''(p_i)/S''(p_j)}$ carries the sign of the jump and the orientations of the thimbles. Cauchy's formula turns this into the large orders of the series:

$$a^{(i)}_k\;\approx\;\sum_j\frac{\kappa_{ij}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\sum_{l\ge0}a^{(j)}_l\,\frac{\Gamma(k-l)}{A_{ij}^{\,k-l}},$$

the sum over the critical points that $J_i$ runs into. The late terms of each series are determined by the early terms of the series of its neighbours, and the singularities of the Borel plane are where they show: this is resurgence [@ecalle1981; @berry1991]. The line in the chart is this formula with the first three coefficients of each neighbour's series.

Smoothing the jump

The integral over a fixed path does not jump; only its expansion does. Summed to its smallest term, the series of $J_i$ leaves a remainder of the size of $\mathrm{e}^{-A_{ij}/\hbar}$. Across the Stokes ray this remainder is the jumping term times a factor that rises smoothly from $0$ to $1$,

$$\tfrac12\bigl(1+\operatorname{erf}\sigma\bigr),\qquad\sigma=-\frac{\mathrm{Im}\,(A_{ij}/\hbar)}{\sqrt{2\,\mathrm{Re}\,(A_{ij}/\hbar)}},$$

over an angle of about $\sqrt{2|\hbar/A_{ij}|}$, as Berry found [@berry1989]. Seen from the series, the Stokes phenomenon is a smooth, rapid change, which is the narrower the smaller $\hbar$.
